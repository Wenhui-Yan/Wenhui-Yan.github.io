Kernel-Driven BRDF Models: From RTLT to RTCLSR

2026-01-22 · Remote Sensing

The Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Function (BRDF) describes how a surface reflects light as a function of illumination and viewing geometry. Kernel-driven semi-empirical models decompose the BRDF into a weighted sum of basis functions (kernels), each capturing a distinct scattering mechanism. This note walks through the two models I use most: RTLT and its hotspot-corrected variant RTCLSR.

The Kernel-Driven Framework

The general form is:

R(θs, θv, φ) = fiso + fvol · Kvol + fgeo · Kgeo

where fiso, fvol, and fgeo are the fitted weights, and the two kernels represent volumetric (canopy multiple scattering) and geometric-optical (shadow/crown) contributions respectively. MODIS MCD43 uses this framework with the RossThick volumetric kernel and the LiSparse-Reciprocal geometric kernel (b/r = 1, h/b = 2).

Understanding the Scattering Angle

The scattering angle ξ is the angle between the solar and viewing directions. Its value drives the dominant scattering regime:

  • Small ξ (backscatter / hotspot): the observer looks back toward the sun. Shadows are hidden behind crowns, so reflectance peaks sharply.
  • Large ξ (forward scatter): shadows are exposed, crowns appear darker, and reflectance decreases.
  • Nadir observation: a special case where the viewing zenith angle (VZA) is zero; the hotspot contribution is averaged out across all azimuth angles.

Two Calculation Paths: BRF vs. Albedo

The same kernel weights support two distinct outputs depending on what you integrate over:

  • BRF (Bidirectional Reflectance Factor): evaluate the model at a specific (θs, θv, φ). This is geometry-driven — "how bright does the surface look from this direction?"
  • Black-sky albedo: integrate BRF over all viewing directions for a fixed solar zenith angle. The result depends only on SZA; VZA and RAA drop out. The hotspot leaves only a weak average contribution after integration.

1. RTLT Model

The RossThick-LiSparse (RTLT) model pairs the RossThick volumetric kernel with the LiSparse geometric kernel. The LiSparse kernel includes a shadow-overlap function that accounts for mutual shadowing between crowns. This combination works well across most viewing geometries but systematically underestimates reflectance near the hotspot direction, where the shadow-hiding effect is strongest.

2. RTCLSR Model: Hotspot Correction

RTCLSR replaces the standard RossThick kernel with a corrected version that adds an exponential hotspot factor, allowing the model to reproduce the sharp reflectance peak near the backscatter direction. The correction multiplier takes the form:

KvolCLSR = KvolRT · (1 + c1 · exp(−ξ / c2))

where c1 controls the hotspot amplitude and c2 controls its angular width. The recommended band-specific values derived from POLDER data are:

  • Band 1 (Red): c1 = 0.7, c2 = 3.1
  • Band 2 (NIR): c1 = 0.6, c2 = 3.2
  • Band 3 (Blue): c1 = 0.5, c2 = 3.2

A constant offset is also applied to keep the nadir (0, 0, 0) reflectance equal to fiso, ensuring consistency when plugging MODIS MCD43 weights directly into the corrected kernel. If you use the MODIS weights without re-inverting, the error is generally within an acceptable range — but re-inverting with the RTCLSR kernel gives better results.

Practical Notes for MODIS MCD43

MODIS MCD43 products are generated with RTLT (LiSparse-Reciprocal, b/r = 1, h/b = 2). When applying RTCLSR corrections using the published MCD43 weights directly, the nadir offset ensures the isotropic term remains consistent. For studies that require accurate hotspot-direction reflectance — such as BRDF normalization of SIF or vegetation indices to a common geometry — re-inverting with RTCLSR is the more rigorous path.
